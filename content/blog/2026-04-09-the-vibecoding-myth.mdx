You've seen the posts. "Shipped a full app in 3 hours." "Built this over the weekend." Vibe coding culture runs on these posts. The screenshots, the threads, the engagement. Fast builds are becoming the norm.

I'm not here to tell you those posts are fake. I don't think they are. But there's a version of the story they're not telling.

<Divider />

The part they skip is the part I spend most of my time in.

It's the one debugging session that turns into three debugging sessions. The feature you thought would take an afternoon that's still not right two days later. The architectural decision you make confidently on Tuesday and quietly walk back on Friday. None of that makes the highlight reel because it doesn't have a clean ending. It doesn't compress into a tweet.

Budget Buddy is a calendar-based budgeting app I've been building. The core concept isn't complicated: a calendar view of your money, transaction entries, some structure around recurring expenses. But recurring transactions were deceptively simple. The kind of problem that doesn't show its complexity until you're building it. Getting the logic right — handling edge cases, making sure the data model didn't collapse under its own assumptions — took significantly longer than I'd planned for. And none of that made for a good post.

<Divider />

Stackd was different. An editorial link-in-bio tool that I _was_ actually able to prototype in a few hours. That part of the "built it fast" story? True.

But it wasn't ready to share for another week. There's a gap between something working and something being ready, and it's not a small one. That gap is where most of the decisions live. It's where you find out what you didn't think through. The fast part is real. It's just not the whole picture.

I think about those two projects when I see "shipped this in a day" posts. Sometimes the timeline is accurate. Sometimes the prototype was fast and the rest of the work just isn't mentioned. Neither one makes the person dishonest. But the framing creates an impression that doesn't match how building actually works.

<Divider />

Building in public is only valuable if it's honest. That's not a moral claim. It's a practical one.

When the only version of building you see online is the fast, clean, highlight-reel version, it warps your sense of what normal looks like. You debug for three hours and wonder if you're slow. You spend a week on something that should've taken two days and wonder if you're bad at this. You're not. You're just doing the part that doesn't get posted.

The honest version matters because it shows the part that actually takes the longest. Not the highlight reel. Just the real, raw process.

That's worth more than the built-in-a-day post.
